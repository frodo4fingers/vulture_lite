# Evidence and safety basis

Last reviewed: 2026-07-31.

## Claim boundary

Vulture Lite is a behavioral reminder. It does not measure sitting, posture,
hydration, eye health, fatigue, fitness, or work performance. It cannot know
whether a user has already moved away from the screen.

The app therefore makes modest claims:

- changing activity and replacing some sedentary time with movement is
  reasonable public-health and occupational-health guidance;
- brief breaks can reduce fatigue and support vigor;
- light walking has stronger acute cardiometabolic evidence than standing
  still;
- exact reminder intervals are configurable product defaults, not clinical
  thresholds;
- eye, water, coffee, and movement prompts are optional cues rather than
  treatment or intake prescriptions.

## Evidence summary

### Interrupting sedentary work

The World Health Organization recommends limiting sedentary time and replacing
it with physical activity of any intensity. It does not prescribe one exact
break cadence.

The UK Health and Safety Executive recommends short, frequent display-screen
breaks or changes of activity and says duration and frequency depend on the
work. It gives 5–10 minutes each hour as an example and recommends moving,
stretching, or changing posture. OSHA similarly suggests a five-minute break
from computer tasks each hour, which may include looking away, stretching,
standing, or walking.

Experimental systematic reviews report that physical-activity interruptions
to prolonged sitting improve acute post-meal glucose and insulin responses
compared with uninterrupted sitting. Reviews comparing standing and light
walking generally find larger acute effects from walking. These studies do not
establish one optimal cadence or prove long-term disease prevention.

### Micro-breaks and work

Albulescu et al. (2022) defined micro-breaks as pauses under ten minutes. Their
meta-analysis found improved vigor and reduced fatigue on average. Performance
effects varied: break duration and task type mattered, and short breaks did not
consistently improve demanding cognitive work. Included studies did not show a
general performance penalty from taking a break.

### Eye comfort

The American Optometric Association recommends the 20-20-20 rule, regular
blinking, appropriate viewing distance, and rest breaks for digital eye
strain. The exact 20-minute/20-second schedule should not be presented as a
proven treatment. Wilkins et al. (2023), a small controlled study of 30 young
participants performing a 40-minute tablet task, found no significant symptom
or performance effect from 20-second breaks every 5, 10, or 20 minutes.

Vulture Lite therefore labels the default as an eye-comfort cue, lets users
change it, and avoids claims about treating dry eye or improving vision.

### Hydration and coffee

Fluid needs vary with body size, diet, activity, environment, health
conditions, pregnancy, and medication. The app does not set a daily target or
tell users to drink despite thirst or medical advice. “Water” is a convenience
prompt only.

“Make tea or coffee” is framed as a reason to step away, not a recommendation
to consume caffeine. Users can remove it from the activity rotation.

### Guided movements

The movement instructions and doses come from NHS or CCOHS public guidance.
A workplace-exercise systematic review found possible reductions in
musculoskeletal pain, but heterogeneity and risk of bias prevent firm treatment
or prevention claims. Individual movements are included because they are
conservative, traceable options for varying activity, not because each one has
been shown to prevent injury.

## Defaults are product choices

The balanced plan uses:

- 20 seconds of eye comfort every 20 minutes;
- 2 minutes of movement every 30 minutes;
- a water cue every 60 minutes;
- a 5-minute longer reset every 90 minutes;
- a 90-second bundling window for nearby reminders;
- a 10-minute snooze;
- a 30-minute main pause action.

No source validates that complete set as a universal prescription. It is a
low-friction starting point spanning commonly cited occupational guidance and
the short activity protocols used in experimental studies.

## Movement safety

Use a stable, non-wheeled chair and a clear floor area. Move slowly, breathe
normally, and stay within a comfortable range. Do not bounce, force a joint, or
continue through pain.

Stop for pain, severe discomfort, dizziness, chest discomfort, unusual
breathlessness, numbness, or loss of balance. New one-sided calf pain or
swelling is not ordinary stiffness and may need urgent assessment.

People with an injury, recent surgery, osteoporosis, significant balance
impairment, or another condition affecting exercise should ask a qualified
clinician which movements are suitable.
